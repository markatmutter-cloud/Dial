## Model context

The Omega Speedmaster sits at the centre of Omega's modern identity — a chronograph launched in 1957 for motorsport use that became the watch NASA flight-qualified for crewed spaceflight in 1965 and the first wristwatch worn on the lunar surface in 1969. Its origin is a racing instrument: the first wristwatch to put a tachymeter scale on the bezel (Omega initially called it the 'Tacho-Productometer') rather than on the dial, alongside a Seamaster and Railmaster launched the same year as part of a 1957 family of professional tool watches. The space story was not engineered by Omega: astronaut Wally Schirra wore his personal CK2998 on Mercury-Atlas 8 in October 1962, and only after Ed White's 1965 Gemini 4 spacewalk photographs reached Life magazine did Omega's US distributor (Norman M. Morris) realise their watch had been in space. NASA engineer James Ragan's procurement, begun late 1964, sent a specification sheet to Longines, Omega, Rolex and Hamilton; Omega was the only entrant to survive Ragan's qualification regime (thermal vacuum, shock, vibration, pure-oxygen, humidity, decompression) and was certified on 1 March 1965. The 105.012 and 145.012 are the core 'Moonwatch' references built around calibre 321 with the asymmetric lyre-lug case — Armstrong's confirmed Apollo 11 watch was a 105.012 (serial 24002981) and Omega Museum research with Ragan has since concluded Aldrin's was also a 105.012, overturning earlier 145.012 attributions. Demand for the watch is built on this dual identity — racing instrument and verified spaceflight object — reinforced by the Apollo 13 burn-timing story, an unbroken visual identity since the 1960s, and Omega's continued investment in the line (calibre 321 revival, 3861 Master Chronometer, X-33, Speedy Tuesday, Alaska Project homages, MoonSwatch).

## Agreements & conflicts (5)

- **Length of NASA qualification testing** — Monochrome's account is sourced directly from Ragan and Omega Museum archives and should be treated as the authoritative timeline; older marketing-flavoured retellings should be paraphrased with care.
  - Monochrome/Ragan: testing ran October 1964 to March 1965 (roughly four and a half months).
  - Popular accounts repeated in Bailey's, Wristbuddys and others compress or extend the timeline ('three years of testing', 'series of rigorous tests' starting in the early 1960s).
- **Reference of Aldrin's Apollo 11 Speedmaster** — Monochrome's piece is explicitly a correction; cite it as the current best understanding while acknowledging the legacy claim.
  - Monochrome (with Omega Museum and Ragan): Aldrin's watch was a 105.012, same reference as Armstrong's. Aldrin's watch is now lost.
  - Earlier widely repeated attribution (referenced by Monochrome as the older speculation): a 145.012.
- **Why Armstrong left his Speedmaster in the LM** — The malfunctioning-LM-timer explanation is the most commonly cited operational reason; Bailey's wording is loose and should not be treated as evidence Armstrong wore the watch on the surface.
  - Wristbuddys: Armstrong left his as a backup inside the lunar module.
  - Thingap: Armstrong left his because the LM's internal electronic timer had malfunctioned, so his Speedmaster served as the backup timer.
  - Bailey's: implies Armstrong wore his Speedmaster down the ladder.
- **Year/reference Schirra wore on Mercury-Atlas 8** — Consensus is CK2998; Bailey's phrasing is consistent but imprecise.
  - Monochrome and Hodinkee: a CK2998.
  - Bailey's: describes it as a '1959 Omega Speedmaster' and '2nd generation Speedmaster' which corresponds to the CK2998 family but is loosely worded.
- **Apollo 15 — why Scott wore a Bulova** — NY Post hedges with 'the story goes', so treat the crystal-loss explanation as the widely-told version rather than a documented fact.
  - NY Post: 'the story goes that the crystal top fell off his Speedmaster'.
  - Wired (white Speedmaster article): notes Speedmaster crystals could be damaged under extreme conditions, supporting the popular account.

## Module candidates — future nodes (12)

- **CK2915** — The 1957 broad-arrow origin reference with steel tachymeter bezel — Hodinkee's Reference Points gives enough sub-reference detail (2915-1, -2, -3 transitional) to support a dedicated page.
- **CK2998** — The reference Schirra wore in space — black bezel, alpha hands, eight sub-references (-1 through -62). Arguably the archetype of the modern Speedmaster shape.
- **105.003 'Ed White'** — The straight-lug calibre 321 reference, the form Ed White wore on the first US spacewalk — distinct from later asymmetric Moonwatches and a strong collector node.
- **145.022** — The long-running calibre 861 reference that succeeded the 105.012/145.012, with multiple dial generations and the famous Apollo XI tribute variant — Revolution flags this as the heart of one major collector's hoard.
- **BA145.022 (1969 gold Apollo XI)** — The original 1,014-piece gold Apollo 11 commemorative with burgundy bezel and onyx markers, presented to astronauts and Nixon — directly recreated in 2019, so it bridges vintage and modern.
- **Alaska Project** — Multi-iteration NASA-commissioned prototype programme (titanium case, red outer shell, white dial, capsule hands; later bead-blasted Alaska II that flew on Soyuz). Wired piece gives the full design rationale.
- **Silver Snoopy / Apollo 13 commemoratives** — NASA awarded Omega the Silver Snoopy for Apollo 13; multiple Snoopy Speedmaster editions (2003, 2015, 2020) form a coherent sub-collection. Hodinkee's 'How Snoopy Ended Up On The Speedmaster' is the anchor article.
- **Speedmaster X-33** — 1990s analog-digital quartz designed with space agencies, issued to astronauts through Shuttle/ISS/Artemis era — its own technology line and now back in current production.
- **Speedmaster Professional 3861** — The 2021 Master Chronometer update — Co-Axial, silicon hairspring, METAS, thinner case, new bracelet, updated caseback engraving. Distinct enough mechanically to warrant its own modern-reference page.
- **Speedy Tuesday** — The Robert-Jan Broer / Fratello-originated Instagram tradition that became two limited editions (2017, 2018) — a notable case of community shaping product.
- **MoonSwatch** — The 2022 bioceramic Omega x Swatch collaboration extends the Speedmaster identity outside Omega's normal price band; the Mission to Mars piece directly references the Alaska Project.
- **Calibre 321 revival** — Omega's 2019/2020 reintroduction of the column-wheel calibre 321 in platinum and steel — directly tied to the vintage 105.012/145.012/105.003 collecting universe.
