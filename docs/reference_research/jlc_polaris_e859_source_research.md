# Jaeger-LeCoultre Memovox Polaris E859 — Source-Derived Research Notes

## Executive summary

The **Jaeger-LeCoultre Memovox Polaris ref. E859** is one of the most important vintage Jaeger-LeCoultre sports watches: a large, automatic, alarm-equipped diving watch built around the Memovox concept and adapted for underwater use. It combines a **42mm stainless-steel compressor case**, **three crowns**, **internal rotating dive-time bezel**, **automatic calibre K825 alarm movement**, date, and a distinctive **resonance caseback** designed to make the alarm audible and perceptible underwater.

Jaeger-LeCoultre’s own Collectibles page describes the Memovox Polaris as a 42mm steel Memovox watch with substantial water resistance, excellent legibility, black dial, large luminescent indexes, three crowns, and a resonance caseback. JLC states that the watch was equipped with automatic **calibre 825** with alarm and date display, and that its patented Compressor case delivered water resistance to **200 metres**.

The reference is generally associated with production between **1965 and 1971**, with **1,714 total examples** often cited. Phillips, Christie’s, Hodinkee, Le Monde Edmond/Mstanga, and other sources repeat or support this number. There were also approximately **50 prototypes** in 1962/1963 that were not commercially sold, according to Phillips and collector-source accounts.

The most important distinction for collectors is between the early **1965** version and the later **1968-style** version. Dial signatures also matter: **LeCoultre** generally indicates the U.S. market, while **Jaeger-LeCoultre** generally indicates Europe. Phillips, Christie’s, Hodinkee, and Le Monde Edmond all support this market distinction.

## Why the Polaris E859 matters

The E859 is not just “a Memovox with a dive bezel.” It is a purpose-built attempt to make the mechanical alarm useful underwater. The core design problem was simple: a diver could visually time a dive with an internal rotating bezel, but JLC also wanted an **auditory and tactile alarm** that could be heard and felt underwater.

Phillips describes the E859 as JLC’s response to the growing popularity of sports diving: an oversized automatic waterproof diver’s wristwatch with an alarm function, using both visual timing through the inner bezel and auditory timing through the alarm. Phillips also explains that the patented caseback was designed to optimize alarm transmission underwater: the outer caseback has 16 holes, while the inner caseback seals and protects the movement.

JLC’s own description supports the same reading. It presents the Polaris as a watch purposefully designed as a diving companion, with a black dial, large luminescent indexes, three crowns, and resonance caseback.

The result is a watch that sits at the intersection of:

- **JLC alarm-watch history** — part of the Memovox lineage.
- **1960s dive-watch experimentation** — compressor case, internal bezel, large luminous dial.
- **Functional case engineering** — resonance caseback to transmit the alarm underwater.
- **Collector scarcity** — low production, multiple dial/caseback variants, and many restored or incorrect examples in circulation.

## Historical context

### Memovox lineage

The Memovox was introduced in 1950 and became one of Jaeger-LeCoultre’s signature complications. Phillips describes the name as meaning “voice of memory” and notes that the alarm was set using a central disc with an arrow operated by a second crown.

The Polaris belongs to the sport/tool-watch branch of the Memovox family. JLC had already explored the idea of a diving alarm with the **Memovox Deep Sea Alarm ref. E857** in 1959. Le Monde Edmond’s interview with Mstanga states that the E857 was the first automatic diving alarm wristwatch and that the later Polaris developed from the idea of creating a diver’s watch with an alarm function.

### Development of the Polaris

Collector-source accounts place the earliest E859 prototypes around **1962/1963**, with around 50 prototypes produced and not sold publicly. Phillips also states that during 1962 and 1963, 50 prototypes were produced but never sold to the public.

The production E859 is usually said to have debuted in **1965**. Fratello describes the E859 as debuting in 1965 after years of development and prototyping, with a 42mm case, 200m water resistance, and a triple caseback architecture.

## Production numbers and variants

### Total production

The most repeated figure is **1,714 examples**. This number is cited by Phillips, Hodinkee, Christie’s, Le Monde Edmond/Mstanga, and multiple dealer/collector sources.

Phillips’ Watch Auction One lot says that an Extract from the Archives confirmed 1,714 Memovox Polaris wristwatches manufactured between 1965 and 1971. Other Phillips lots describe the E859 as made in 1,714 total examples.

### Collector-source production breakdown

Le Monde Edmond’s interview with Mstanga gives a more detailed breakdown:

- **50 prototypes in 1963**, reportedly not commercialized and not counted in the 1,714.
- **500 watches in 1965**.
- **1,214 watches in the 1968 version**, including 1,150 produced in 1968 and 9 produced in 1967.
- **55 units produced in 1970** with the controversial “Calypso” hands version.

Because this is a collector-source breakdown rather than an official JLC production table published directly on JLC’s site, it should be presented as a collector-source figure, albeit one that is widely repeated and supported by archive-extract references.

## Main variants

## 1. 1965 Polaris

The 1965 E859 is the early commercial version and is generally treated as rarer than the more familiar 1968-style watch. Phillips & Blackbird: SPORTS sold a 1965 E859 with blank guarantee, JLC green presentation box, manual booklet, additional bracelet, and an Extract from the Archives confirming production in 1965.

Important traits:

- Early dial layout compared with later 1968 examples.
- 42mm stainless-steel case.
- K825 automatic alarm movement.
- Three-crown compressor layout.
- Highly collectible when paired with original accessories or archive extract.

## 2. 1967 / transitional examples

Phillips has sold a 1967 E859 catalogued as **Memodate, Polaris** with the caseback stamped **6-67** and an archive extract confirming production in 1967. Phillips notes that the **MEMODATE** inscription below the alarm-setting triangle is a very rare feature, where the designation **MEMOVOX** is normally found.

This is important for a reference page because it shows that not every correct E859 dial says Memovox in the same way. The Memodate variant should be treated as a rare sub-variant, not automatically dismissed as incorrect.

## 3. 1968 Polaris

The 1968-style Polaris is the iconic version most collectors picture today: large black dial, big luminous trapezoid-style markers, baton hands, internal rotating bezel, three crowns, and pierced resonance caseback. JLC’s modern Polaris collection and Tribute models draw heavily from this 1968 design language.

Phillips, Hodinkee, Christie’s, and JLC all use 1968 examples as the standard visual and technical reference point.

## 4. LeCoultre-signed U.S. examples

U.S.-market watches are generally signed **LeCoultre** rather than Jaeger-LeCoultre. Phillips, Christie’s, Hodinkee, Hairspring, Bulang & Sons, and Le Monde Edmond all support this distinction.

These watches are important because the U.S. “LeCoultre” dial is not a lesser or incorrect version. It is a legitimate market variant. Hodinkee specifically notes that the LeCoultre-signed U.S. Polaris was not the style remade in the modern Tribute models, which adds extra collector interest.

## 5. Jaeger-LeCoultre-signed European examples

European-market watches generally carry the full **Jaeger-LeCoultre** signature. Phillips describes a European E859 signed Jaeger-LeCoultre on the dial and notes that the U.S. market used the simplified LeCoultre brand.

## 6. Tropical dial examples

Tropical or brown-dial examples appear periodically in auction and dealer listings. Phillips sold a 1968 E859 with a brown tropical dial at Geneva Watch Auction SIX for CHF18,750. Hairspring and other dealers have highlighted tropical E859 examples with strong dial ageing.

Tropicality should be treated carefully: attractive ageing can be valuable, but it also increases the importance of verifying originality, lume, printing, and whether the colour change is natural rather than damage or relume-related.

## 7. “Calypso hands” / 1970 version

The Le Monde Edmond/Mstanga breakdown refers to **55 units produced in 1970** with controversial Calypso hands. Because this is a more specialist collector point and not as broadly documented in auction house summaries, it should be used cautiously and attributed to the collector-source breakdown.

## Technical architecture

## The movement: calibre K825 / 825

The E859 is powered by the automatic Jaeger-LeCoultre **calibre K825 / 825**, a 17-jewel automatic alarm movement with date. JLC’s Collectibles page states that the watch is equipped with automatic calibre 825 with alarm function and date display. Phillips lots repeatedly list the E859 with automatic calibre K825 / 825.

The movement is significant because the E859 applies JLC’s alarm-watch expertise to a diving context. The alarm is set through the central alarm disc/triangle and crown system, while the internal bezel provides visual timing.

## Three crowns

The E859 has three crowns:

1. One crown for winding/setting the time and date.
2. One crown for rotating the internal dive-time bezel.
3. One crown for winding and setting the alarm.

Phillips describes the three crowns as cross-hatched Super Compressor-style crowns, with one controlling time setting, one controlling the inner bezel, and one controlling the alarm disc.

## Compressor case

JLC’s own Collectibles page states that the Polaris used a patented Compressor case with a spring-loaded compression system and a flat rubber gasket held under permanent tension. As pressure increased, the case became more resistant.

Fratello attributes the case collaboration to EPSA / Ervin Piquerez S.A., the specialist compressor-case maker. Phillips also describes the watch as using Super Compressor-style construction.

## Resonance caseback and alarm audibility

The caseback is one of the defining features. The system is generally described as multi-layered:

- An inner caseback seals the movement.
- A resonating layer or structure helps transmit the alarm.
- An outer pierced back, with **16 holes**, allows the sound to escape and helps the wearer hear or feel the alarm underwater.

JLC, Phillips, Christie’s, Hodinkee, Le Monde Edmond, and Fratello all describe this resonance/audibility concept.

## Design notes

## Dial and legibility

The Polaris E859 is defined by legibility: black dial, large luminous hour markers, central alarm indicator, baton or other variant-specific hands, and an internal rotating dive bezel. Christie’s emphasizes the watch’s black luminous dial and its refined but functional aesthetic.

The later 1968-style dial is the most recognizable configuration. Fratello notes that the later generation shifted from glossy to matte black dials, introduced characteristic trapezoid luminous tritium markers, and standardized baton hands.

## Case and proportions

At 42mm, the E859 was exceptionally large for the 1960s. That is a major part of its modern appeal. Christie’s describes the case as 42mm with sleek faceted lugs. JLC calls the watch “generous” in size, and Phillips repeatedly lists 42mm dimensions.

## Crystal

Phillips makes an important collector point: original rounded domed crystals are rare, and many surviving examples have later service crystals with sharper edges. This is an inspection point worth including on any buyer checklist.

## Bracelet and straps

The watch is commonly associated with rubber/Tropic-style straps and, in some cases, optional **JB Champion** bracelets. Le Monde Edmond/Mstanga states that the E859 was delivered with a 22mm Tropic-style plastic strap or, on request, with a JB Champion steel bracelet for the U.S. market. Phillips has sold examples with JB Champion bracelets, box, certificates, guarantees, and additional bracelets.

## Authenticity and due diligence checklist

The E859 is heavily collected and technically specific. It is also a watch where incorrect parts, service components, refinished dials, relume, and assembled examples can materially affect value.

## 1. Dial signature and market logic

- **LeCoultre** generally indicates U.S. market.
- **Jaeger-LeCoultre** generally indicates European market.
- Both can be correct.
- The signature should align with the watch’s other evidence: movement import marks, caseback, archive extract, dial layout, and production year.

## 2. Dial text and variant logic

- Most examples are expected to read **Memovox**.
- Rare **Memodate** examples exist and have been documented by Phillips.
- The exact dial style should match the production period: 1965 vs 1967/1968 vs later/Calypso variants.

## 3. Lume

Check:

- Large tritium plots.
- Consistency between dial and hands.
- Natural ageing vs relume.
- Whether lume is intact or degraded.

Phillips and dealer descriptions frequently emphasize original and fully intact luminous material when examples are especially strong.

## 4. Crowns

The three crowns should be correct cross-hatched compressor-style crowns. Replacement crowns materially affect originality and value.

## 5. Crystal

Phillips notes that original rounded domed crystals are rarely seen and that many watches have service crystals with sharper edges. This is an important visual and value point.

## 6. Caseback

Confirm:

- Outer pierced back with 16 holes.
- Inner waterproof caseback.
- Correct engravings and patent/compressor markings where expected.
- Production/date stamps where present.

## 7. Movement

The movement should be automatic calibre K825 / 825 with alarm and date. Check movement number against archive extract if available.

Note: one Phillips NY Watch Auction X listing appears in search/opened text as “cal. K285,” likely a typo or cataloguing inconsistency, because the established movement for the E859 is K825/825 and Phillips’ essay and other lots support the K825. Treat any “K285” mention with caution and verify directly from images or an archive extract.

## 8. Accessories

Premium accessories include:

- Extract from the Archives.
- Original box.
- Original guarantee / blank guarantee.
- Manual booklet.
- Original or period-correct Tropic strap.
- JB Champion bracelet.
- Original JLC buckle.

## Suggested Watchlist reference-page framing

### Short intro draft

The Jaeger-LeCoultre Memovox Polaris E859 is one of the great strange sports watches of the 1960s: a 42mm automatic alarm diver built around the idea that a dive watch could be timed not only visually, but audibly and tactilely underwater. Its three-crown compressor case controls the time, the internal dive bezel, and the Memovox alarm, while the pierced 16-hole resonance caseback helps the alarm sound travel through water and wetsuit. Produced in low numbers across 1965 and 1968-style variants, and signed either LeCoultre for the U.S. market or Jaeger-LeCoultre for Europe, the E859 is a cult JLC: technically serious, visually modern, and much stranger than the usual Submariner-era dive-watch template.

### Positioning tags

- Vintage JLC sports watch
- Memovox alarm diver
- Compressor case
- Internal rotating bezel
- Three-crown diver
- 1960s oversized case
- 42mm vintage diver
- Calibre K825 / 825
- LeCoultre U.S. market
- Jaeger-LeCoultre European market
- Alarm complication

## Collector interpretation

The E859 appeals to collectors who like purpose-built oddness rather than generic sports-watch design. It is a serious diver, but it is not a Submariner clone. It is an alarm watch, but not a dress Memovox. It is a compressor-case watch, but with a specifically JLC twist: the alarm is not decorative, it is part of the dive-timing logic.

For Watchlist/recommender use, the Polaris E859 is a strong bridge reference because it connects multiple collecting paths:

- **From JLC dress watches** → into technical JLC sports watches.
- **From Memovox** → into alarm-tool watches and unusual complications.
- **From Submariner / Seamaster / Fifty Fathoms** → into less obvious 1960s dive-watch engineering.
- **From EPSA compressor cases** → into Longines Legend Diver, Enicar Super Compressor, Universal Genève Polerouter Sub, and other internal-bezel divers.
- **From vintage tool watches** → into complication-led tool watches, where the function changes the architecture of the case.

The watch is most interesting for collectors who value **engineering specificity**: the E859 exists because JLC tried to solve a real underwater-use problem in a very JLC way.

## Open questions / areas for further work

1. **Production breakdown verification**  
   The 1,714 total is well supported, but the exact breakdown by year and sub-variant is mostly from collector-source material and archive-extract references. A stronger reference page would ideally cite JLC archive extracts or The Collectibles book directly.

2. **Dial variant taxonomy**  
   The page should eventually include side-by-side images of 1965, 1967/Memodate, 1968, LeCoultre, Jaeger-LeCoultre, tropical, and Calypso-hand examples.

3. **Crystal originality**  
   Phillips’ note about original domed crystals vs service crystals deserves visual examples.

4. **Caseback engravings**  
   A proper guide should include caseback engravings, inner/outer back details, patent references, and date stamps.

5. **Fake / assembled-watch risk**  
   The E859 is valuable enough that incorrect or assembled examples are a real issue. A buyer guide should include red flags, especially around dial printing, lume, hands, crowns, caseback, and movement/case-number consistency.
